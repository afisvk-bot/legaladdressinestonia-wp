Short answer: the essentials in 60 seconds

The law does not require an Estonian OÜ to hire an accountant. A management board member may keep the books personally, but is personally responsible for them: for complete records, filing deadlines and the annual report. For a company with a simple model and few transactions, doing your own accounting is realistic. For a company with VAT, payroll and multi-currency payments, it quickly becomes more expensive than an accountant.

Key facts for 2026:

  • Bookkeeping is mandatory from the date of registration, even if the company has no activity.
  • Books are kept using double-entry on the basis of source documents, in euros.
  • Every transaction needs a document: an invoice, receipt, contract or statement. Documents are kept for 7 years.
  • Monthly: TSD by the 10th when paying individuals or distributing profit; KMD by the 20th if the company is VAT-registered.
  • Annually: the annual report within 6 months after the end of the financial year, usually by 30 June, including a zero report.
  • There is a free tool: e-arveldaja, the state accounting software for start-ups and small companies, which also prepares the annual report.
  • From October 2026 TSD is submitted as data from accounting software, and KMD will follow from 2027.
  • The cost of mistakes: EMTA interest of 0.06% per day on unpaid tax and register fines of up to €3,200, including personally for the board member.

Below: when doing your own accounting makes sense, what exactly has to be done every month and every year, and where saving on an accountant turns into additional tax assessments.

The fork: three questions that decide the format

Whether you can do the books yourself or should hand them to an accountant depends on three facts about your company.

Question 1. Is the company registered for VAT?

If not, monthly reporting comes down to TSD, and only in months when you pay individuals. If yes, you need a KMD with the KMD INF annex every month, and a VD report when selling to businesses in the EU. This is the main dividing line: VAT requires the correct classification of every transaction. More in our article VAT registration in Estonia: when you need it.

Question 2. Who does the company pay?

If no money is paid to anyone (no salary, no board member fee, no dividends), no TSD is needed. If you pay yourself a board fee or hire employees, you get tax calculations, the employment register and a TSD every month.

Question 3. How many transactions are there and where do they come from?

A dozen bank transactions a month on one account is an hour of work. Hundreds of payments through Stripe, Wise and PayPal in different currencies mean reconciliations, exchange rates and fees, which turn into errors without experience.

Practical conclusion: doing your own accounting is realistic for a company without VAT, without employees and with few transactions. The more "yes" answers to these three questions, the more expensive it is to save on an accountant.

What the law requires

The Accounting Act is the same for a company with or without an accountant. The only difference is who does the work: responsibility always lies with the management board.

Basic accounting rules

  • Double-entry. Every transaction is recorded as a debit and a credit to accounts. An "income and expenses spreadsheet" is not enough for an OÜ.
  • Source documents. Every entry is based on a document: a sales invoice, supplier invoice, receipt, contract, or a bank or payment service statement.
  • Currency: euro. Transactions in other currencies are converted at the rate on the transaction date.
  • Retention. Documents and ledgers are kept for at least 7 years; electronic storage is allowed.
  • Accounting policies. The company sets its accounting methods: how revenue is recognised, how fixed assets are recorded, which exchange rate is used. For small companies this is a short document, but it must exist.

What the annual report includes

The annual report consists of a management report and financial statements: balance sheet, income statement, notes and, for some companies, a cash flow statement. Small companies prepare a simplified report. A board member signs it, the shareholders approve it, and it is then filed through the e-Business Register. More in our article annual report in Estonia.

Tools

Doing your own accounting requires accounting software. The state e-arveldaja is free and designed for start-ups and small companies: sales invoices, expenses, bank statements, data for tax returns and the annual report. Commercial software such as Merit, SmartAccounts and others offers more automation: bank and payment service imports and direct TSD submission to EMTA. An Excel spreadsheet does not replace software: it does not keep double-entry books and does not produce reports.

When doing your own accounting is not enough

  • The company has become VAT-registered. Every transaction needs a decision on the rate, place of supply and right to deduct. EMTA sees errors by matching KMD INF with counterparties. See monthly accounting and VAT returns (KMD).
  • Buying services from foreign suppliers. Advertising, hosting and SaaS subscriptions from abroad may require registration as a limited VAT payer, with no threshold. This is the most common mistake in DIY accounting.
  • You have employees. Employment register, basic exemption, minimum social tax, sick leave: see accounting and payroll services.
  • Many payment services and currencies. Stripe and Paddle payouts are net of fees; revenue is calculated per transaction, and exchange rates on the date of each transaction.
  • You need statements for a bank, an investor or your home country's tax office. Self-prepared statements often fail third-party review.
  • You do not live in Estonia. Board fees, dividends and your country's CFC rules require knowledge of two tax systems at once. See accounting for e-residents.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping an "income and expenses spreadsheet" instead of double-entry books.
  • Recording Stripe or PayPal payouts as revenue instead of gross sales before fees.
  • Paying personal expenses from the company account: these are non-business expenses taxed at 22/78.
  • Not charging VAT on advertising and subscriptions from foreign suppliers.
  • Applying reverse charge without checking the customer's VAT number in VIES.
  • Filing a "zero" annual report when there were transactions on the account.
  • Forgetting TSD in a month when a board fee or dividends were paid.
  • Not declaring a capital contribution in TSD, and later being unable to return it tax-free.
  • Keeping documents only in email and losing them when switching services.

Three real-life scenarios

Scenario 1. Freelance consultant, e-resident from Portugal. Clients are EU companies, turnover €50,000, no VAT registration, no payouts to herself during the year. Solution: DIY accounting in e-arveldaja is realistic. About 20 transactions a month, a monthly bank reconciliation and an annual report once a year. The main risk is foreign software subscriptions, which may require registration as a limited VAT payer.

Scenario 2. Shopify store with Stripe and PayPal. Sales to consumers in several EU countries, OSS, VAT registration in Estonia. Solution: DIY accounting quickly hits the wall of reconciling hundreds of transactions, different countries' rates and refunds. It makes more sense to outsource; see OSS and IOSS in Estonia.

Scenario 3. SaaS start-up with two employees in Tallinn. Salaries, board fees, VAT, grants, investors. Solution: DIY accounting is not justified. Errors in TSD and KMD cost more than an accountant, and investors need statements that withstand scrutiny.

The common denominator: whether you can keep the books yourself depends not on turnover but on the number and complexity of transactions.

What changes next

EMTA is moving reporting to data transfer from accounting software. From October 2026 TSD is submitted this way, from 2027 KMD (merged with the VD report), and in 2028 the remaining TSD annexes. By 2030 the EU will introduce mandatory e-invoicing and near real-time reporting. For DIY accounting this means one thing: spreadsheets and manual entry are on their way out, and software with a machine interface becomes the bare minimum. How to choose an accountant with this infrastructure is covered in how to choose an accountant in Estonia.

Mini glossary

RPS (Raamatupidamise seadus): the Accounting Act.

Double-entry: recording every transaction as a debit to one account and a credit to another.

Source document: a document that supports a transaction: invoice, receipt, contract, statement.

TSD: monthly income and social tax return.

KMD: monthly VAT return.

Majandusaasta aruanne: annual report.

e-arveldaja: free state accounting software for small companies.

What to do next

  1. Answer three questions: do you have VAT, who do you pay, how many transactions per month.
  2. Choose accounting software: e-arveldaja or a commercial one with bank imports.
  3. Set your accounting policies: revenue recognition, exchange rates, fixed assets.
  4. Reconcile your bank and payment services and collect documents every month.
  5. Set reminders: the 10th and 20th of every month, and 30 June for the annual report.
  6. As soon as VAT, employees or foreign payments appear, weigh the cost of an accountant against the cost of mistakes.
