This article is based on the EU Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA, 2023/1114), the Estonian Crypto-Asset Market Act (krüptovaraturu seadus), the Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing Prevention Act (RahaPTS), and guidance from the Financial Supervision Authority (Finantsinspektsioon), the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) and the Tax and Customs Board (EMTA). Licensing requirements change, so check fi.ee and fiu.ee before you apply.

Short answer: the essentials in 60 seconds

Estonia remains one of the most convenient EU jurisdictions for crypto businesses, but the 2026 rules are very different from the era of "quick FIU licences". You can still register an OÜ for a crypto project online in a few days. Providing crypto services to clients, however, now requires a CASP authorisation under MiCA from the Financial Supervision Authority.

Key facts for 2026:

  • The regulator is Finantsinspektsioon (EFSA). The national Crypto-Asset Market Act entered into force on 1 July 2024, and MiCA has applied in full since 30 December 2024.
  • Old FIU licences are no longer valid. No new VASP authorisations were issued after 30 December 2024, and the transition period ended on 1 July 2026.
  • Minimum CASP capital is €50,000, €125,000 or €150,000 depending on the class of services.
  • The application fee is €3,000; in practice the procedure takes 4–6 months, and up to a year in complex cases.
  • Real presence in Estonia is required: a registered office, a management board, a compliance officer permanently based in Estonia, and a working AML system.
  • Taxes: retained profit is not taxed; dividends are taxed at 22/78 of the net amount (22% of the gross) in 2026; VAT is 24%.
  • DAC8 applies from 1 January 2026: crypto service providers collect client and transaction data and report it to the tax authority.
  • Not every crypto company needs a licence. Blockchain development, investing own funds and consulting without handling client assets work with a regular OÜ.

Below: how to tell whether your project needs a licence, what the law requires, what it costs and which mistakes most often lead to refusal.

The fork: three questions that decide the format

The word "crypto" in a business name says nothing about regulation. Three facts decide the company format.

Question 1. Do you provide crypto services to third parties?

MiCA lists ten services: custody of client crypto-assets, operating a trading platform, exchanging crypto-assets for funds or other crypto-assets, executing and transmitting orders, placing, transfer services, advice and portfolio management. If you do even one of these for clients, you need a CASP licence. If you write smart contracts to order, invest your own funds or sell software, that is ordinary OÜ activity. More on the CASP licence under MiCA page.

Question 2. Where are your clients?

A CASP licence obtained in Estonia is valid across the EU through passporting. If your clients are only outside the EU, the Estonian company is still supervised, because the service is considered to be provided from Estonia. Regulators read "reverse solicitation" narrowly, and it cannot serve as a business model.

Question 3. Do you touch clients' money or keys?

Custody of client assets is the class with the strictest requirements for capital, asset segregation and IT security. Non-custodial solutions, where keys stay with the user, may fall outside MiCA altogether, but this should be confirmed by legal analysis before launch, not after the regulator's first request.

Practical conclusion: first classify every product feature against the MiCA list. Everything else depends on it: capital, time to launch, budget and team.

What the law requires

For a licensed crypto business, the requirements come from three layers: MiCA, the Estonian Crypto-Asset Market Act and anti-money laundering legislation.

Company requirements

  • Legal form and seat. An OÜ or AS with a registered office in Estonia. A virtual address is not enough for a licensed CASP: you need a place where management actually works. For an unlicensed OÜ, a legal address in Estonia is sufficient.
  • Management board. As a rule, at least two members of good repute with proven experience in finance or crypto-assets; at least one of them is responsible for AML compliance.
  • Compliance officer. Permanently based in Estonia; their qualifications are assessed by the FIU.
  • Own funds. Not below the minimum for the class or one quarter of annual fixed overheads, whichever is higher.
  • Documents. A three-year business plan, programme of operations, risk management rules, AML/KYC policy, conflicts of interest policy, business continuity plan, description of IT systems and cybersecurity, and complaints handling procedure.
  • Audit. An audited annual report is submitted to the Financial Supervision Authority.

Real presence requirements are covered in detail on the substance in Estonia page.

AML, Travel Rule and reporting

A crypto service provider remains an obliged entity under RahaPTS: client identification, transaction monitoring and reporting suspicious transactions to the FIU. The Travel Rule (EU Regulation 2023/1113) has applied since 30 December 2024: every crypto-asset transfer carries the originator's and beneficiary's data. From 2026, under the DAC8 directive, providers collect tax data on users and transactions; the first report for 2026 is filed with EMTA in 2027.

Taxes for a crypto company

A crypto company pays tax under the general Estonian rules. Profit retained in the company is not taxed, including profit from selling crypto-assets. Tax arises on distribution: dividends, hidden profit distributions and non-business expenses. More in the article Estonian corporate tax. Under EU Court of Justice case law (Hedqvist, C-264/14), exchanging cryptocurrency for fiat money is generally VAT-exempt, while fees for other services, development and consulting are taxed under the general rules. Accounting for crypto-assets requires valuation on the date of each transaction and proof of the source of funds, so your accountant must work with exchange and wallet exports.

When a regular OÜ is not enough

  • You hold client assets or keys. This is a class 2 custody service, even if custody is only a side feature of your app.
  • You exchange crypto for clients, for money or other tokens, including "silent" swaps inside an app.
  • You receive and transmit orders or match buyers and sellers on your platform.
  • You manage other people's crypto portfolios or give personal investment recommendations.
  • You operated under an FIU licence. After 1 July 2026, companies without a MiCA authorisation cannot continue providing services. If an application has been filed and is still pending, the activity is not considered illegal, but new clients cannot be onboarded. More on the transition from the FIU to the Financial Supervision Authority.
  • You issue tokens and offer them to the public in the EU. You need a MiCA white paper, and stablecoins (ART and EMT) require a separate authorisation.

Common mistakes

  • Registering a company "for a licence" without analysing which MiCA services are actually provided.
  • Relying on a nominee board and a virtual office: the Financial Supervision Authority checks real management from Estonia.
  • Filing with template AML policies unrelated to your product and risks.
  • Failing to prove the source of founders' funds and capital, one of the most common causes of delays.
  • Assuming that non-EU clients exempt you from supervision.
  • Continuing to onboard clients under an old FIU licence after 1 July 2026.
  • Applying for a bank account without first explaining the flow of money and crypto-assets.
  • Keeping crypto records in a spreadsheet without exchange and wallet exports and rates on the transaction date.
  • Taking profit out through non-business expenses: they are taxed like dividends.

Three real-life scenarios

Scenario 1. A Web3 studio of three developers. Writes smart contracts and dApps for clients in the EU and the US, gets paid in euros and USDC. Solution: a regular OÜ via e-Residency, a legal address and a contact person. No licence is needed: the studio does not provide crypto services, it sells development. It is important to account correctly for stablecoin payments and to invoice VAT according to the place-of-supply rules.

Scenario 2. An exchange app for EU clients. Exchanging crypto for euros and holding users' assets. Solution: a class 2 CASP licence, capital from €125,000, an office and a compliance officer in Estonia, and a budget for legal preparation, audit and IT security. A realistic time to launch is 6–12 months. An alternative at the start is working through a licensed partner.

Scenario 3. A former FIU licence holder. Since 2021 the company operated as an exchange under an Estonian VASP licence. Solution: if no application was filed with the Financial Supervision Authority before 1 July 2026, crypto services must stop, client relationships must be settled, and a path must be chosen: a new CASP application, a partnership, or moving to another EU country and passporting back.

The common denominator: regulation is determined not by the tokens on your balance sheet but by the services you provide to clients.

What changes next

2026 is the year of the final move to MiCA: national licences are a thing of the past, and the EU market is consolidating around providers with full authorisations. As of March 2026, the Estonian Financial Supervision Authority had not yet granted a single CASP authorisation, while 67 providers from other EU countries operated in Estonia under a passport. This means the queue is long and the bar for application quality is high. Tax transparency is tightening at the same time: DAC8 gives tax authorities data on residents' crypto transactions, and from 2027 they will be matched with tax returns. For new projects the conclusion is simple: budget for regulation and real presence in Estonia from day one. More about crypto projects on the crypto and Web3 projects page.

Mini glossary

MiCA (Markets in Crypto-Assets): EU Regulation 2023/1114 on markets in crypto-assets.

CASP (crypto-asset service provider): a provider of crypto-asset services authorised under MiCA.

VASP: a virtual currency service provider under the former Estonian FIU regime.

Finantsinspektsioon (EFSA): the Estonian Financial Supervision Authority, which grants and supervises CASP authorisations.

FIU (Rahapesu Andmebüroo): the Financial Intelligence Unit, which supervises AML compliance.

Travel Rule: the obligation to send originator and beneficiary data with every crypto transfer.

DAC8: the EU directive on the exchange of tax information on crypto-assets.

Substance: a company's real presence: office, management and staff in the country of registration.

What to do next

  1. Describe your product feature by feature and map each one to the MiCA list of services.
  2. Choose the format: a regular OÜ, your own CASP licence or working through a partner.
  3. If no licence is needed, register a company in Estonia with a legal address and a contact person.
  4. If a licence is needed, put the capital, board, compliance officer and office in Estonia in place before applying.
  5. Prepare a package for the bank in advance: business model, flow of funds, source of capital; see how to open a bank account for an Estonian company.
  6. Set up accounting that can work with exchange and wallet exports, and a reporting calendar: TSD, KMD, annual report, DAC8.
