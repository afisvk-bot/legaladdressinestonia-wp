## content_blocks_0_content
Payroll in Estonia looks simple: a flat 22% income tax, 33% social tax, one return per month. The mistakes are in the details. The basic exemption applies only when the employee applies for it. Social tax is paid on at least the minimum base, even for part-time work. The first days of sick leave are paid by the employer, not the health fund. And from October 2026 the TSD is submitted as a data feed rather than a form.

The second group of mistakes is organisational. An employee must be entered in the employment register before the first working day, not after the first payslip. A board member fee is taxed differently from a salary. And a "remote employee" abroad is often not Estonian payroll at all, but employer obligations in another country.
<blockquote>We calculate salaries and board member fees, register employment, file the TSD under the new data-based scheme and calculate sick pay, holiday pay and compensation. For small teams we charge a fixed price per employee, with no extra fee per return.</blockquote>

## content_blocks_7_title_right
Key insight: payroll mistakes are in the set-up, not in the rates
## content_blocks_7_text_right
Estonian rates are flat, and that creates a false sense of simplicity. In practice, mistakes happen in five places:
<ul>
<li><b>Basic exemption without an application.</b> The €700 applies only on the employee's application, and only with one employer. If the employee applied with two employers, there will be extra tax to pay at year end.</li>
<li><b>Minimum social tax.</b> For part-time work, social tax is paid on at least €886 per month unless an exception applies to the employee. It is often forgotten for students and second jobs.</li>
<li><b>Sick pay for days 4–8.</b> The employer pays it from its own funds, and Tervisekassa only takes over from day 9. Without it, the payroll for a month with sick leave is wrong.</li>
<li><b>TÖR after the first payslip.</b> The start of work is registered before the first working day. Registering backdated is a breach that shows up in every audit.</li>
<li><b>The old basic exemption formula.</b> From 2026 the exemption is the same for everyone, with no reduction as income grows. Software with the old setting gets every payroll run wrong.</li>
</ul>
<b>Practical takeaway:</b> before the first payroll run we check not only the rate but the applications, the contract type, the TÖR entry and the pension rate of each employee. We do this at onboarding.
## content_blocks_8_title_left
When Estonian payroll is not needed and when it is not enough
## content_blocks_8_text_left
An honest assessment saves more than the service itself.
<ul>
<li><b>The employee lives and works in another country.</b> Their salary is usually taxed there, and the Estonian company may have employer obligations in that country. The Estonian TSD does not cover this: you need local payroll or an EOR.</li>
<li><b>A freelancer invoices you as a business.</b> That is not a salary: they pay their own taxes in their country, and you pay the invoice.</li>
<li><b>The owner takes no payments.</b> If there is no board member fee and no salary, a monthly TSD is usually not filed; monthly accounting and a dividend decision are enough.</li>
<li><b>A foreigner works in Estonia.</b> You need not only payroll and TÖR, but also compliance with the work permit conditions, including the salary threshold. Failing to pay the required salary can lead to a fine of up to €32,000.</li>
<li><b>Options and share bonuses.</b> This is a question of fringe benefit tax paid by the company, and of the three-year rule for options. Simple payroll does not cover it.</li>
</ul>

<h2><b>How payroll works in Estonia: in practice</b></h2>
<h3><b>Calculation order: from gross to net</b></h3>
<ol>
<li>Gross salary under the contract.</li>
<li>Minus the funded pension (pillar II) contribution: 2%, 4% or 6%.</li>
<li>Minus the employee's unemployment insurance: 1.6%.</li>
<li>Minus the basic exemption: €700 if an application exists.</li>
<li>Income tax of 22% on the remainder.</li>
<li>On top of gross, the employer pays 33% social tax and 0.8% unemployment insurance.</li>
</ol>
<h3><b>Employment register (TÖR)</b></h3>
Everyone the company pays for work with a tax obligation in Estonia must be entered in the employment register. The entry is made no later than the start of work: the contract type, the start date and, for an employment contract, the position, the workplace address and the working time. A foreigner without an Estonian personal code can be registered by date of birth for up to five days. The end of work is entered within 10 days. TÖR data is the basis of the employee's health insurance, so a mistake here hits the employee directly.
<h3><b>The TSD return from October 2026</b></h3>
From 1 October 2026, payment data for the former annexes 1 and 2 is sent straight from the accounting software, and EMTA compiles the return itself. The company confirms it by the 10th. The first TSD under the new scheme is for October, due on 10 November 2026. Manual entry in e-MTA remains, the old CSV is accepted until the end of 2027, and annexes 3–8 move to data in 2028.

<h3><b>Board member fee</b></h3>
A fee under a board member contract is subject to 22% income tax and 33% social tax, but not to unemployment insurance. The rule also applies to board members living abroad: payments from an Estonian company for board work are taxed in Estonia. For more on choosing between a fee and dividends, see our article on <a href="{L:844}">corporate tax in Estonia in 2026</a>.
<h3><b>Foreign employees</b></h3>
A non-EU foreigner works in Estonia on the basis of a short-term employment registration or a residence permit for work. The employer confirms that it will pay at least the required salary and can be fined up to €32,000 for a breach. Start-up employees work on simplified terms through the Startup Visa programme.
<h3><b>What to keep</b></h3>
Employment contracts, basic exemption applications, timesheets, payslips and the grounds for payments are kept with the accounting documents for at least seven years.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_0_question
What payroll taxes apply in Estonia?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_0_answer
Employee: 22% income tax after the €700 basic exemption, 1.6% unemployment insurance and 2–6% pillar II pension. Employer, on top of gross: 33% social tax and 0.8% unemployment insurance.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_1_question
How much does an employee cost the company?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_1_answer
At €2,000 gross, the company spends about €2,676 per month. With a 2% pension and a basic exemption application, the employee takes home about €1,658.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_2_question
What is the minimum social tax?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_2_answer
Social tax is paid on at least €886 per month, i.e. at least €292.38, even if the salary is lower. There are exceptions, for example for some second jobs and students.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_3_question
When is the TSD filed?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_3_answer
By the 10th of the month after the payment. From October 2026, payroll data is sent from the software and EMTA compiles a return that the company confirms.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_4_question
When should an employee be entered in the employment register?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_4_answer
No later than the moment they start work. The end of work is entered within 10 days.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_5_question
Is a board member fee taxed if the board member lives abroad?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_5_answer
Yes. A fee for board work in an Estonian company is taxed in Estonia at 22% income tax and 33% social tax. The tax consequences in the country of residence depend on the tax treaty.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_6_question
Who pays for sick leave?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_6_answer
The first three days are unpaid. Days 4–8 are paid by the employer at 70% of average pay, and from day 9 by Tervisekassa.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_7_question
Can I hire someone living in another country through Estonian payroll?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_7_answer
Usually not. Their salary is taxed in the country where they work, and the company has employer obligations there. You need local payroll or an EOR, and we help set it up.
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_8_question
Do I need to file a TSD if no salaries were paid?
## content_blocks_11_faq_items_8_answer
As a rule, no: the return is filed for a month with taxable payments or a profit distribution.
